# Maitland Mobilization Techniques

## Overview

The Maitland Concept, developed by Australian physiotherapist Geoffrey Maitland (1924-2010), is a comprehensive approach to musculoskeletal assessment and treatment that emphasizes passive joint mobilization techniques. It is one of the most widely taught and practiced manual therapy approaches worldwide, known for its systematic assessment process, graded mobilization techniques, and patient-centered clinical reasoning.

The Maitland approach is characterized by its emphasis on continuous assessment and reassessment, attention to detail in examination, and the use of oscillatory passive movements applied at varying grades of intensity and amplitude. The technique is based on sound biomechanical principles combined with clinical reasoning that considers both the patient's subjective experience and objective findings.

## Core Principles

### 1. **Patient-Centered Clinical Reasoning**
- Treatment guided by patient's subjective response
- Continuous reassessment of symptoms and signs
- "Listen to what the patient tells you"
- Balance between subjective and objective findings
- Individual response determines treatment progression

### 2. **Brick Wall Concept**
- **Permeable Brick Wall**: Represents the therapist's theoretical knowledge
- **Impermeable Brick Wall**: Represents the patient's actual presentation
- Therapist must be willing to modify theory based on clinical findings
- Patient's response is the ultimate guide
- Avoid forcing treatment to fit preconceived notions

### 3. **Assessment and Reassessment**
- Continuous assessment throughout treatment
- Immediate reassessment after each technique
- Compare pre- and post-treatment findings
- Modify treatment based on response
- "Assess, treat, reassess" cycle

### 4. **Graded Mobilization System**
- Five grades of mobilization (I-V)
- Each grade has specific indications
- Grades can be combined and modified
- Progression based on patient response
- Precise control of force and amplitude

### 5. **Comparable Sign**
- Identify specific movement or test that reproduces symptoms
- Use as baseline for reassessment
- Must be reliable and reproducible
- Guides treatment selection
- Measures treatment effectiveness

## Theoretical Foundation

### Biomechanical Principles

#### Joint Mechanics
- **Accessory Movements**: Movements that cannot be performed actively (glides, rolls, spins)
- **Physiological Movements**: Active movements (flexion, extension, rotation)
- **Combined Movements**: Combinations of physiological movements
- **Joint Play**: Small movements essential for normal joint function
- **End-Feel**: Quality of resistance at end of range

#### Movement Diagram
- Graphical representation of movement analysis
- Plots pain, resistance, and spasm against range
- Helps determine appropriate treatment grade
- Guides clinical reasoning
- Predicts treatment response

### Neurophysiological Effects

#### Pain Modulation
- **Gate Control Theory**: Large fiber stimulation inhibits pain transmission
- **Descending Inhibition**: Activates pain-inhibiting pathways
- **Endogenous Opioids**: Releases natural pain-relieving substances
- **Psychological Effects**: Reduces anxiety and fear of movement

#### Mechanical Effects
- Increases joint mobility
- Reduces muscle guarding
- Improves synovial fluid circulation
- Stimulates mechanoreceptors
- Maintains cartilage nutrition

## Maitland Grading System

### Grade I
- **Description**: Small amplitude rhythmic oscillations at the beginning of range
- **Amplitude**: Minimal movement
- **Position**: Start of available range
- **Speed**: 2-3 oscillations per second
- **Primary Effect**: Pain relief, neurophysiological
- **Indications**: 
  - Acute pain
  - Severe irritability
  - High pain levels
  - Initial assessment

### Grade II
- **Description**: Large amplitude rhythmic oscillations within the range, not reaching resistance
- **Amplitude**: Larger movement than Grade I
- **Position**: Middle of available range
- **Speed**: 2-3 oscillations per second
- **Primary Effect**: Pain relief, some mechanical effect
- **Indications**:
  - Moderate pain
  - Moderate irritability
  - Stiffness with pain
  - Subacute conditions

### Grade III
- **Description**: Large amplitude rhythmic oscillations into resistance/stiffness
- **Amplitude**: Large movement
- **Position**: Into resistance at end of range
- **Speed**: 2-3 oscillations per second
- **Primary Effect**: Increase mobility, stretch tissues
- **Indications**:
  - Stiffness with minimal pain
  - Chronic conditions
  - Restricted range of motion
  - Low irritability

### Grade IV
- **Description**: Small amplitude rhythmic oscillations into resistance/stiffness
- **Amplitude**: Small movement
- **Position**: At end of available range
- **Speed**: 2-3 oscillations per second
- **Primary Effect**: Increase mobility, neurophysiological
- **Indications**:
  - Stiffness with minimal pain
  - End-range restrictions
  - Chronic conditions
  - Specific joint restrictions

### Grade V (Manipulation)
- **Description**: Small amplitude, high velocity thrust at end of range
- **Amplitude**: Minimal movement
- **Position**: At anatomical limit
- **Speed**: High velocity
- **Primary Effect**: Mechanical disruption, neurophysiological
- **Indications**:
  - Specific joint restrictions
  - Minimal irritability
  - Appropriate patient selection
  - When mobilization insufficient
- **Note**: Requires advanced training and appropriate scope of practice

## Assessment Process

### Subjective Examination

#### Patient Interview
- **Body Chart**: Location, depth, quality of symptoms
- **Behavior of Symptoms**: Aggravating and easing factors
- **24-Hour Pattern**: Morning, day, evening, night symptoms
- **History**: Onset, progression, previous episodes
- **Special Questions**: Red flags, general health, medications
- **Patient's Goals**: Functional limitations, expectations

#### Symptom Characteristics
- **Pain**: Location, intensity, quality, behavior
- **Stiffness**: Morning stiffness, movement-related
- **Weakness**: True weakness vs. pain inhibition
- **Instability**: Giving way, lack of control
- **Functional Limitations**: ADLs, work, recreation

### Objective Examination

#### Observation
- Posture and alignment
- Gait analysis
- Willingness to move
- Protective behaviors
- Structural abnormalities

#### Active Movements
- Range of motion
- Quality of movement
- Pain response
- Willingness to move
- Functional movements

#### Passive Movements
- **Physiological Movements**: Overpressure at end range
- **Accessory Movements**: Joint play, glides
- **Combined Movements**: Multiple directions simultaneously
- **End-Feel Assessment**: Normal vs. abnormal
- **Pain Response**: Location, intensity, behavior

#### Palpation
- Temperature and texture
- Muscle tone and spasm
- Tenderness and pain
- Joint position and alignment
- Tissue quality

#### Neurological Examination
- Sensation testing
- Reflex testing
- Muscle strength
- Neural tension tests
- Coordination

### Movement Diagram Analysis
- Plot pain (P) and resistance (R) against range
- Identify relationship between pain and resistance
- Determine irritability level
- Guide treatment grade selection
- Predict treatment response

## Treatment Techniques

### Spinal Mobilization

#### Cervical Spine
- **Central PA (Postero-Anterior)**: Applied to spinous process
- **Unilateral PA**: Applied to articular pillar
- **Transverse**: Side-to-side gliding
- **Rotation**: Oscillatory rotation
- **Longitudinal**: Cephalad or caudad gliding
- **Combined Movements**: Multiple directions simultaneously

#### Thoracic Spine
- **Central PA**: Spinous process mobilization
- **Unilateral PA**: Transverse process/rib mobilization
- **Transverse**: Lateral gliding
- **Rotation**: Oscillatory rotation
- **Rib Mobilization**: Specific rib techniques
- **Combined Techniques**: Multiple directions

#### Lumbar Spine
- **Central PA**: Spinous process mobilization
- **Unilateral PA**: Transverse process mobilization
- **Transverse**: Lateral gliding
- **Rotation**: Oscillatory rotation
- **Straight Leg Raise Mobilization**: Combined with neural mobilization
- **Flexion/Extension**: Physiological movements

### Peripheral Joint Mobilization

#### Shoulder Complex
- **Glenohumeral Joint**:
  - Inferior glide (for abduction restriction)
  - Posterior glide (for flexion/internal rotation restriction)
  - Anterior glide (for extension/external rotation restriction)
  - Lateral distraction
- **Acromioclavicular Joint**: PA and AP glides
- **Sternoclavicular Joint**: PA, AP, superior, inferior glides
- **Scapulothoracic**: Mobilization of scapular movements

#### Elbow Complex
- **Humeroulnar Joint**: Distraction, medial/lateral glides
- **Humeroradial Joint**: Distraction, compression, AP glides
- **Proximal Radioulnar**: AP and PA glides
- **Combined Techniques**: With physiological movements

#### Wrist and Hand
- **Radiocarpal**: AP and PA glides, radial/ulnar glides
- **Intercarpal**: Specific glides between carpal bones
- **Carpometacarpal**: Distraction and glides
- **Metacarpophalangeal**: Distraction, rotation, glides
- **Interphalangeal**: Distraction and glides

#### Hip
- **Distraction**: Longitudinal traction
- **Inferior Glide**: For flexion restriction
- **Posterior Glide**: For flexion/internal rotation restriction
- **Anterior Glide**: For extension restriction
- **Lateral Glide**: For adduction restriction
- **Compression**: For joint nutrition and proprioception

#### Knee
- **Tibiofemoral**: AP and PA glides, distraction, rotation
- **Patellofemoral**: Superior, inferior, medial, lateral glides
- **Proximal Tibiofibular**: AP and PA glides
- **Combined Techniques**: With flexion/extension

#### Ankle and Foot
- **Talocrural**: AP and PA glides, distraction
- **Subtalar**: Inversion/eversion, distraction
- **Midtarsal**: Specific glides between tarsal bones
- **Tarsometatarsal**: Glides and mobilization
- **Metatarsophalangeal**: Distraction, glides, rotation
- **Interphalangeal**: Distraction and glides

## Clinical Indications

### Spinal Conditions
- Neck pain and stiffness
- Thoracic pain and dysfunction
- Low back pain
- Facet joint dysfunction
- Disc-related pain (appropriate stages)
- Postural disorders
- Spondylosis
- Post-surgical stiffness

### Peripheral Joint Conditions
- Osteoarthritis
- Post-traumatic stiffness
- Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder)
- Post-surgical restrictions
- Ligament sprains
- Joint hypomobility
- Chronic joint pain
- Movement dysfunction

### Neurological Conditions
- Nerve root compression (with caution)
- Neural tension syndromes
- Radiculopathy (appropriate stages)
- Peripheral nerve entrapment

## Contraindications and Precautions

### Absolute Contraindications
- Malignancy affecting treatment area
- Active infection or osteomyelitis
- Acute fractures
- Spinal cord compression
- Cauda equina syndrome
- Vertebrobasilar insufficiency (cervical manipulation)
- Severe osteoporosis with fracture risk
- Acute inflammatory arthritis

### Relative Contraindications
- Pregnancy (certain techniques)
- Anticoagulant therapy
- Hypermobility syndromes
- Acute disc herniation with neurological signs
- Severe pain (modify grade)
- Rheumatoid arthritis (active phase)
- Spondylolisthesis (unstable)

### Precautions
- Elderly patients (osteoporosis risk)
- Post-surgical patients (healing tissues)
- Patients on corticosteroids
- Systemic diseases affecting bone/joints
- Psychological factors (fear-avoidance)
- Litigation/compensation cases

## Evidence Base

### Research Support
- **Spinal Pain**: Moderate to strong evidence for short-term pain relief and improved function
- **Peripheral Joints**: Good evidence for improving range of motion and reducing pain
- **Osteoarthritis**: Moderate evidence for pain relief and functional improvement
- **Post-Surgical**: Evidence supports mobilization for preventing stiffness
- **Chronic Pain**: Moderate evidence when combined with exercise

### Systematic Reviews
- Multiple systematic reviews support joint mobilization for various conditions
- Evidence strongest for neck and low back pain
- Combination with exercise more effective than mobilization alone
- Grade III-IV mobilizations effective for stiffness
- Grade I-II mobilizations effective for pain

### Clinical Effectiveness
- High patient satisfaction
- Immediate effects on pain and range
- Cost-effective intervention
- Low risk when appropriately applied
- Complements other interventions

## Clinical Application Guidelines

### Treatment Planning

#### Determining Treatment Grade
- **High Irritability**: Grades I-II
- **Moderate Irritability**: Grades II-III
- **Low Irritability**: Grades III-IV
- **Pain Dominant**: Lower grades (I-II)
- **Stiffness Dominant**: Higher grades (III-IV)
- **End-Range Restriction**: Grade IV

#### Treatment Dosage
- **Duration**: 30-60 seconds per technique
- **Repetitions**: 2-4 sets per technique
- **Frequency**: 2-3 times per week initially
- **Progression**: Based on reassessment findings
- **Home Program**: Active exercises to maintain gains

#### Progression Criteria
- Reduced pain with movement
- Increased range of motion
- Improved function
- Positive patient feedback
- Objective improvements maintained
- No adverse reactions

### Clinical Reasoning Process

#### Treatment Selection
1. **Identify comparable sign**: Reproducible symptom
2. **Analyze movement diagram**: Pain vs. resistance relationship
3. **Determine irritability**: High, moderate, or low
4. **Select appropriate grade**: Based on analysis
5. **Choose technique**: Accessory or physiological movement
6. **Apply treatment**: Precise technique application
7. **Reassess immediately**: Compare to baseline
8. **Modify as needed**: Based on response

#### Reassessment Markers
- Comparable sign (primary measure)
- Range of motion
- Pain levels (VAS/NPRS)
- Functional activities
- Patient-reported outcomes
- Objective tests

## Clinical Pearls

- **Listen to the patient**: Their response guides treatment
- **Reassess constantly**: After every technique application
- **Start conservatively**: Can always progress, harder to regress
- **Be specific**: Precise technique application is critical
- **Combine with exercise**: Mobilization alone is insufficient
- **Respect irritability**: High irritability requires gentle grades
- **Use comparable sign**: Reliable measure of treatment effect
- **Movement diagram helps**: Visualize pain-resistance relationship
- **Patient education essential**: Understanding improves outcomes
- **Brick wall concept**: Be flexible in clinical reasoning
- **Quality over quantity**: Precise technique beats repetition
- **Immediate effect expected**: If no change, reassess approach
- **Integrate with function**: Transfer gains to daily activities
- **Document thoroughly**: Subjective and objective findings
- **Safety first**: Know contraindications and precautions

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Inadequate assessment**: Rushing to treatment without thorough examination
2. **Ignoring irritability**: Using too aggressive grade for irritable condition
3. **Poor technique**: Imprecise hand placement or force application
4. **Not reassessing**: Missing opportunity to modify treatment
5. **Treating too long**: Excessive treatment can increase irritability
6. **Ignoring subjective**: Focusing only on objective findings
7. **No home program**: Failing to maintain treatment gains
8. **Inappropriate grade**: Not matching grade to condition
9. **Missing red flags**: Failing to identify serious pathology
10. **Passive treatment only**: Not incorporating active exercise

## Case Example: Chronic Shoulder Pain

### Presentation
- 52-year-old female
- 6-month history of shoulder pain
- Difficulty reaching overhead and behind back
- Pain at night
- Previous cortisone injection with temporary relief

### Subjective Examination
- **Body Chart**: Lateral shoulder pain, occasional radiation to mid-arm
- **Aggravating**: Reaching overhead, sleeping on affected side
- **Easing**: Rest, avoiding overhead activities
- **24-Hour Pattern**: Worse in evening and night
- **History**: Gradual onset, no specific injury

### Objective Examination
- **Active ROM**: Flexion 130° (pain at end), Abduction 110° (pain), IR limited
- **Passive ROM**: Similar to active with capsular end-feel
- **Accessory Movements**: 
  - Inferior glide: Restricted, painful
  - Posterior glide: Restricted, stiff
- **Comparable Sign**: Active abduction to 110° reproduces pain

### Movement Diagram Analysis
- Pain (P1) begins at 90° abduction
- Resistance (R1) begins at 100°
- Both increase together to end range
- Moderate irritability

### Treatment
**Session 1**:
- Grade II inferior glide (pain relief)
- Grade III inferior glide (increase mobility)
- Reassess: Abduction improved to 125°, pain reduced
- Home program: Pendulum exercises, active-assisted ROM

**Sessions 2-4**:
- Progressed to Grade III-IV inferior and posterior glides
- Added Grade III distraction
- Combined with active ROM exercises
- Strengthening exercises introduced

### Outcome
- Full pain-free ROM achieved after 6 sessions
- Returned to all functional activities
- Maintenance program for home
- Discharged with self-management strategies
